IBS & low FODMAP

Bloating: possible causes and when to get help

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

When your abdomen feels bloated, it is tempting to blame the pasta, your cycle, tight trousers or the fact that you ate lunch at all. Sometimes food contributes. Sometimes the whole day matters. Bloating may occur with diagnosed IBS, but it can also be a reason to speak to a doctor rather than try another food list.

This guide looks at the wider picture: what bloating can mean, factors described by the Dutch medical sources and when to seek help. There is a separate article on bloating after eating for the meal-specific questions.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

What people mean by bloating

The word can describe a full or tight feeling, an abdomen that looks larger, rumbling, wind, burping or discomfort. MDL Fonds describes bloating and visible abdominal swelling as common with IBS. You can feel bloated without the bowel being particularly full; the sensation and visible swelling do not always match.

MDL Fonds also discusses the role of the diaphragm. The way the diaphragm and abdomen respond to gas can affect how far the abdomen protrudes. This helps explain why a swollen-looking abdomen does not necessarily mean you have eaten too much. Holding in wind may add pressure or cramps; passing wind is normal.

Bloating is a symptom, not a diagnosis. IBS, constipation, your cycle and other causes can look similar from the outside. A doctor can assess the distinction. See what IBS is and how it is assessed.

Possible contributors

There is no single explanation for every bloated abdomen. Several factors can overlap.

Constipation. Thuisarts describes pain when the bowel contains hard, dry stool and is moving less effectively. Fibre and enough fluid are among the initial recommendations; a strict exclusion diet is not automatically the next step. See the basics of eating with IBS.

Gas production. The Netherlands Nutrition Centre explains that certain carbohydrates, known as FODMAPs, are broken down by bacteria in the large intestine, which can produce gas. People differ in sensitivity to the resulting stretching. That mechanism is not a reason to remove a whole food group on your own. Our FODMAP explainer covers the terms.

Eating pace and drinks. The Dutch Association of Dietitians discusses chewing and eating pace. Thuisarts notes that some people notice more symptoms with fizzy drinks. These are possible factors to investigate, not proof that a particular drink caused your bloating.

Stress. Thuisarts and MDL Fonds describe stress as something that can trigger or worsen gut symptoms. This does not make the symptoms imaginary or your fault. Read more about IBS and stress.

A condition other than IBS. Similar symptoms can occur with other conditions, including coeliac disease. A doctor decides whether testing is needed. Do not try to diagnose the cause yourself by excluding gluten. A gluten-free diet is not routinely required for IBS alone.

Bloating in the evening or nearly every day

MDL Fonds describes IBS-related bloating as often increasing through the day and after meals. By evening, several meals, drinks and a busy schedule may all be part of the picture. The last plate of food is not necessarily responsible for everything you feel.

If symptoms are mainly associated with meals, our article on bloating after eating discusses possible triggers and next steps. Night-time symptoms, particularly diarrhoea, deserve medical discussion rather than being dismissed as the end of a long day.

If bloating happens almost every day and affects your life, speak to a doctor, especially if you do not have a diagnosis. A few days of notes about food, fullness and timing can help the appointment. BellyBliss can be a place for food and gut-feeling entries, but a pattern in a diary is an observation, not an explanation.

Bloating and the menstrual cycle

Thuisarts notes that IBS is more common in women. Bloating can also increase around menstruation in people without IBS. MDL Fonds discusses hormonal changes that may slow or speed bowel activity. See IBS and periods for what to record across a few cycles without immediately changing your whole diet.

Endometriosis is a different condition that can cause symptoms resembling IBS, particularly around menstruation. A doctor needs to assess whether it may be involved. Severe pain or bleeding that prevents you getting through the day should not simply be attributed to your gut.

When to contact a doctor

Intermittent bloating can be part of diagnosed IBS. However, Thuisarts lists changes that need assessment: blood in or around stools, suddenly passing stools much more or less often, a marked change in stool consistency, frequent urges without passing anything, unexplained weight loss or bowel symptoms first appearing at age 50 or older.

MDL Fonds also highlights fever. The Dutch general practice guideline discusses night-time diarrhoea, persistent changes and relevant family history, such as inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease or some cancers at a young age in a parent or sibling. These features should not be assumed to be ordinary IBS.

For severe abdominal pain together with fever, seek urgent medical advice. In the Netherlands, the cited guidance advises contacting a GP or out-of-hours GP service immediately; elsewhere, use the appropriate local urgent-care service.

Contact a doctor if you are worried or symptoms are dominating your days. You do not need to get your diet “right” before asking for an assessment. A blog and an app cannot examine you.

Frequently asked questions

What does a bloated abdomen mean?

Usually it describes a full or tight feeling, sometimes with visible swelling, wind, rumbling or burping. The feeling can occur without the bowel being full. Bloating is a symptom, not a diagnosis.

Is bloating always IBS?

No. Constipation, gas, your menstrual cycle and stress may contribute, and other conditions can cause similar symptoms. A doctor diagnoses IBS and decides whether further investigation is needed.

When should I seek medical advice about bloating?

Contact a doctor about blood in stools, unintended weight loss, fever, night-time symptoms, sudden bowel changes or symptoms starting after age 50. Also seek advice if you are worried or symptoms affect daily life without an established diagnosis.
