IBS & low FODMAP

FODMAPs in Dutch supermarkets: labels, portions and lists

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

A FODMAP list is a starting point, not a ready-made shopping basket. You might be standing in Albert Heijn or Jumbo with a jar of pasta sauce and a list on your phone, wondering why two brands have quite different ingredients.

This guide is specifically about shopping in the Netherlands. It is not a meal plan, a product-by-product guarantee or a promise that a food list will resolve symptoms. A doctor diagnoses IBS, and a low FODMAP approach needs a dietitian’s guidance.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

A FODMAP list is not a personal shopping list

Monash University explains that the approach is more complex than avoiding everything labelled high and choosing everything labelled low. Foods differ in concentration, and preparation, ripeness and portion size matter. Laboratory analysis is needed to establish FODMAP content.

A Dutch supermarket’s own-brand product is therefore not automatically equivalent to an example in an international guide. Recipes and ingredients can differ even when the product names look similar.

A list can still help you understand the categories. Monash’s overview includes carrot, potato, kiwi and eggs among lower alternatives, and onion, garlic, apple, pear, honey and ordinary cow’s milk among higher examples. These are general examples, not an individual permission or exclusion list. Read what FODMAPs are for the terminology.

Portions matter in the supermarket too

Monash describes a dose-related response: a food may cause symptoms only beyond an amount that differs between people. A smaller serving can therefore be different from the portion you would normally put on your plate.

Its discussion of serving size uses wholemeal wheat bread as an example in which one slice and two slices can receive different ratings. The point is that the serving changes the assessment, not that this establishes a safe bread allowance for you. Monash discusses similar portion effects for foods including wheat pasta, sweet potato, avocado, chickpeas and sweetcorn.

A green mark applies to the assessed amount, not the entire packet. Ratings and serving information can be updated. Check a current reliable source with your dietitian rather than treating an old list as a permanent threshold.

Reading a Dutch label

Low FODMAP is not a common certification mark on the Dutch products discussed here. Monash has an official certification scheme for products tested in its laboratory at the serving stated on the packaging. This guide does not establish that any specific Albert Heijn or Jumbo range is certified.

For Dutch supermarket products, the back of the pack is the practical starting point. Ingredient order generally reflects weight. Monash notes that a higher-FODMAP ingredient near the beginning often matters more than the same ingredient towards the end, with exceptions such as onion, garlic, inulin, FOS and GOS, where small quantities can still contribute.

“High fibre”, “sugar-free” and gut-health marketing do not establish FODMAP suitability. Gluten-free is a separate question: gluten is a protein, while fructans are the FODMAP carbohydrates discussed in wheat. A gluten-free bar or pasta can still contain relevant ingredients, such as soy flour. Thuisarts does not recommend a gluten-free diet for IBS alone.

Ingredient names to recognise at Albert Heijn and Jumbo

Use the same reading approach for an own-brand product and a premium brand. Sauces, soups, seasoning mixes, plant-based drinks and snack bars are worth checking. The food-label guide explains more of the detail.

  • Onion and garlic: Dutch names include uienpoeder (onion powder), uienextract (onion extract), knoflookpoeder (garlic powder) and knoflookextract (garlic extract). Vegetable powder may also include onion or garlic.
  • Added fibres: inulin, chicory root and chicory root fibre, FOS and GOS. On Dutch labels, look for inuline and cichoreiwortelvezel. These names do not all belong to the same FODMAP subgroup.
  • Fructose-related ingredients: honey, fructose syrup and apple or pear juice concentrate. A syrup’s name alone does not quantify the FODMAP content; keep uncertainty rather than treating every syrup as equivalent.
  • Lactose-related ingredients: milk, milk powder, milk ingredients, whey, yoghurt and cream. Lactose-free dairy is different from avoiding all dairy; hard cheese is among the lower alternatives in Monash’s overview.
  • Polyols: sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol, maltitol and isomalt, sometimes listed using an E-number. They often occur in sugar-free gum and sweets. Some packs carry a warning about a laxative effect.
  • Pulses in less obvious forms: chickpea flour, soy flour and hummus, often with garlic. Monash distinguishes drinks made from whole soybeans from those made using soy protein.

The Dutch words aroma and natuurlijk aroma mean flavouring and natural flavouring. They may leave you unable to establish whether onion or garlic is involved. That is uncertainty, not an automatic red or green light. Two jars in the same category may be different.

A starting set of examples, not a guarantee

These categories reflect examples described by Monash as lower alternatives in assessed portions. They are not exhaustive and do not replace a dietitian’s advice.

  • Vegetables: carrot, potato, cucumber, lettuce, aubergine, green pepper, green beans and courgette.
  • Fruit: kiwi, mandarin, orange and pineapple. Apple, pear and mango appear among higher examples in the same overview.
  • Dairy and alternatives: hard cheese, lactose-free milk and soy-protein-based drinks. Almond drink is also mentioned as a lower alternative; check the whole ingredient list.
  • Protein foods: eggs, plain meat, fish or poultry without marinade, firm tofu and tempeh.
  • Grains: oats, rice, quinoa and plain rice cakes. Monash gives sourdough spelt bread as an example, but sourdough products are not all equivalent.
  • Nuts and seeds: macadamias, peanuts, walnuts and pumpkin seeds, with portion size in mind. Cashews and pistachios appear among higher examples.
  • Sweet ingredients: table sugar and maple syrup as lower examples; honey and some sugar-free confectionery as higher examples.

Plain fresh foods can be easier to interpret than a processed product with many ingredients. No specific Albert Heijn or Jumbo product range is endorsed here. For a less label-focused approach to the whole shop, read grocery shopping with IBS.

Work with a dietitian

Thuisarts, MDL Fonds, the Netherlands Nutrition Centre and the Dutch Association of Dietitians recommend professional support for a low FODMAP approach. Excluding many foods alone can reduce nutrient or energy intake and make eating more worrying.

The approach is temporary, intended to explore which groups affect you. It is not a permanent restricted way of eating or a weight-loss plan.

If one or two foods repeatedly seem involved, Thuisarts suggests seeing what happens when you reduce them for a few weeks. If many foods are involved or eating a balanced diet is becoming difficult, ask for help. The three phases include expanding the diet again. In the supermarket, keep reading labels, considering portions and recording your experience. A pattern starts a conversation; it does not prove a cause.
