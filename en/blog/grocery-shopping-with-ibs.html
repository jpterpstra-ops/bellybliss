IBS & low FODMAP

Grocery shopping with IBS, without fearing every aisle

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

A supermarket can feel loud, bright and demanding when your gut has been uncomfortable all afternoon. You want something for dinner, not a research project between the freezer aisle and the checkout. With IBS, every packet can start to feel like a question: can I eat this?

The honest answer is that tolerance varies. MDL Fonds lists dairy, wheat, caffeine, some fruit and vegetables, juices, alcohol, fizzy drinks, sweeteners and chewing gum as possible symptom triggers. They do not affect everyone in the same way, and this is not a list to exclude all at once. A dietitian can help investigate what matters for you.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

Start with enough food and regular meals

Before distrusting an entire aisle, consider the basics. The Dutch general practice guideline describes the value of a more balanced eating pattern: enough variety and fibre, rather than little food all day followed by a large evening meal. The UK lifestyle advice it discusses includes eating slowly and regularly, drinking enough and considering caffeine, alcohol, fizzy drinks and foods that personally cause gas.

This is not yet a low FODMAP programme. It is the foundation. The guideline recommends a dietitian when several foods seem to trigger symptoms or the diet becomes unbalanced.

Thuisarts suggests that if one or two foods repeatedly seem to cause problems, you can see what happens when you have less of them for a few weeks. If the list is long, or eating well and getting enough fibre is difficult, make an appointment. Cutting out more and more foods can mean too little energy or nutrition, and greater anxiety about eating.

Read the ingredients, not just the front of the pack

“High fibre”, “high protein” and “no added sugar” are marketing messages, not a prediction of your gut’s response. Look at the ingredients on the back, generally listed in order of weight. A short list is not automatically better and a long one is not automatically worse. You are looking for information, not grading the food.

Our FODMAP explainer introduces the terminology. If you shop in the Netherlands, the Albert Heijn and Jumbo guide looks more closely at Dutch labels and portions. These local examples do not imply that recipes or labelling practices are identical in other countries.

Names worth recognising include onion and garlic in different forms, inulin, chicory root fibre, FOS, honey, apple juice concentrate and sweeteners such as sorbitol or mannitol. Monash highlights that small amounts of concentrated onion, garlic or inulin can matter even near the end of an ingredient list. See hidden FODMAPs on food labels for more examples.

Gluten-free is a separate issue. Thuisarts does not recommend a gluten-free diet simply because someone has IBS. A gluten-free bar may still contain chicory fibre or polyols. A shelf logo does not make it the right option for your symptoms. Follow medical advice if you have a separate diagnosis such as coeliac disease.

A practical trip around the supermarket

You do not need to solve every aisle. For an ordinary weekday shop, these are ways to make the choices more manageable.

  • Start with familiar foods. Rice, potatoes, oats, eggs, unseasoned meat or fish, or vegetables you already tolerate can provide a starting point. Familiarity is useful information; it is not a universal list of safe foods.
  • Check sauces and soups separately. Onion powder, garlic and cream can be less obvious here. A simpler preparation may be easier to understand than a long list of unfamiliar ingredients.
  • Read dairy labels instead of avoiding the whole section. Lactose is one FODMAP; it does not mean all dairy is the same. Hard cheese differs from a large glass of milk. If dairy seems to be an issue, discuss it with a dietitian.
  • Include drinks in the picture. Fizzy drinks, a large juice, several coffees or chewing gum can be relevant for some people with IBS. A drink alongside a meal is another factor to note.
  • Give bars and “healthy” snacks a second look. These can contain inulin or polyol sweeteners. Being hungry at the checkout makes careful label reading harder.

This is not a meal plan. BellyBliss does not provide a diet plan or recipes, and this article does not either. The aim is to leave the shop with food you can work with, without feeling that your basket has to be perfect.

If you are already working with a dietitian

Use shopping to put your agreed plan into practice, rather than treating each package as a second opinion. In the first weeks of a low FODMAP approach, higher-FODMAP foods are replaced with alternatives under guidance. That does not mean removing everything you enjoy forever.

The three phases include reintroduction. Do not stay in the restrictive stage simply because shopping feels uncertain.

Take a photo of a confusing label or note what you bought and how you felt later. Bringing one unclear product to the conversation is more useful than changing ten things at once and being unable to tell what made a difference.

When the shopping basket is not the problem

Shopping stress is unpleasant. Blood in your stools, unexplained weight loss or fever should not be explained away by a new brand of crackers. Contact a doctor. See the warning signs discussed in bloating after eating.

If food itself has become frightening, that is a reason to ask for help. Thuisarts describes how fear of reintroducing food can become a serious problem, including disordered eating. A doctor or dietitian can help; a stricter list is not the answer.
