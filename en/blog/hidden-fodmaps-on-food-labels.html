IBS & low FODMAP

Hidden FODMAPs on food labels: the ingredient names to check

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 6 min read

You put back the bread, skipped the onion and still felt bloated after lunch. It happens a lot during a low FODMAP trial, but symptoms alone cannot tell you what caused them. Checking the pack can help identify ingredients to discuss. FODMAPs rarely appear on a label as "FODMAP". They hide behind names like inulin, dehydrated vegetables, E420 or apple juice concentrate.

This guide walks through the ingredient names worth recognising, why their position on the list matters (and when it doesn't), and what is different on labels in the UK, the US and Australia. It is a label-reading aid, not a diagnosis or a diet plan. If you are trying a low FODMAP approach, do it with a doctor (GP) and ideally a FODMAP-trained dietitian [1][2].

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

First, how ingredient lists work

In the UK, the US and Australia, ingredients are listed in descending order by weight. Something near the start of the list makes up more of the product than something at the end. Monash University, which developed the low FODMAP diet, suggests being particularly careful when a typically high FODMAP ingredient is among the first few, and less worried when it appears near the end of a long list [3].

There are important exceptions to that rule, which is exactly why these ingredients are called "hidden".

The exceptions: small amounts that still count

According to Monash, garlic, onion (including powders), and purified FODMAPs such as inulin, fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) and galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS) can contribute a lot of FODMAPs even in small amounts [3]. So "onion powder" at the end of a crisp seasoning is not automatically harmless just because it is listed last.

The ingredient names to recognise

Below are common label names grouped by FODMAP type, based on Monash's label-reading and food-processing guidance [3][4].

Fructans and GOS (oligosaccharides)

  • Garlic and onion in any form: garlic powder, garlic salt, garlic extract, onion powder, onion salt, dehydrated onion, shallot.
  • Dehydrated vegetables / vegetable powder: often include garlic or onion [3].
  • Inulin, chicory root, chicory root fibre (fiber), chicory root extract, FOS, oligofructose: concentrated fructans, frequently added to high-fibre bars, "gut health" yoghurts, protein products and some gluten-free breads [3][4].
  • Wheat and rye, especially when they are a main ingredient.
  • Chickpea flour, soy flour, Jerusalem artichoke [3].

Excess fructose

  • Honey, agave syrup, high fructose corn syrup, crystalline fructose, fruit sugar.
  • Fruit juice, fruit juice concentrate and dried fruit, particularly when they are a main ingredient [4].

Lactose

  • Milk, milk powder, milk solids, whey and similar dairy ingredients can contribute lactose. How much depends on the ingredient and the amount, so the presence of milk alone does not tell you whether a serving is high in lactose [3].

Polyols (sugar alcohols)

Often used in "sugar-free" or "no added sugar" products such as chewing gum, mints, sweets and protein bars [3]:

Vague names: "spices", "seasoning" and "natural flavourings"

Some words simply don't tell you enough. "Spices", "seasoning", "flavourings" or "natural flavourings" may or may not include garlic or onion. A UK-based IBS dietitian puts it plainly: you can't tell from the label alone, and you would need to ask the manufacturer [5]. Treat these as unknown, not as safe and not as high.

What is different in the UK, US and Australia

United Kingdom

Allergens are emphasised on UK labels, usually in bold. That helps with milk and wheat, but garlic and onion are not among the 14 regulated allergens, so they are not required to be highlighted as allergens [6]. Celery is an allergen, which sometimes catches people out when scanning a stock cube label.

United States

US labels name the nine major food allergens, including milk and wheat; garlic and onion are not on that list [7]. Under US labelling rules, powdered onion and garlic powder must be declared by their common name rather than hidden as "spice" [8]. However, a "natural flavor" can still be an extract derived from vegetables [8], and Monash notes that on USDA-regulated meat and poultry labels, "natural flavor" may include garlic or onion powder [3]. If you see "natural flavor" on a savoury product, treat it as unknown.

Australia

Australian labels use number codes for additives (e.g. 420 for sorbitol) and follow Food Standards Australia New Zealand allergen labelling rules [9]. As in the UK and US, garlic and onion are not mandatory allergens. Australian shoppers can also look for certification logos from Monash University and FODMAP Friendly on tested products; the Monash programme lists certified products and serving sizes [10].

Why a label can't give you the full picture

Ingredient lists show what is in a product, not how many FODMAPs end up in your serving. Monash points out that processing changes FODMAP content: firm tofu and silken tofu have almost identical ingredient lists, but the lab results differ considerably [11]. Serving size matters too: a food can be fine in a small portion and a problem in a larger one [11]. Only laboratory testing measures FODMAP content directly [4].

So a label is best read as a set of flags, not a verdict:

  1. Possible concerns: garlic, onion and added inulin deserve attention even near the end of a list. Honey, polyols and other ingredients also depend on the product and serving.
  2. Depends on amount: wheat, milk, fruit ingredients near the end of the list.
  3. Unknown: "spices", "seasoning", "natural flavourings", cut-off or blurred text.

A practical routine in the shop

  • Look at the first three ingredients, then scan the whole list for the "exception" ingredients above.
  • Check the serving size you will actually eat.
  • Look for a certification logo if your country uses them.
  • If something stays unclear, write it down rather than guessing, and discuss it with your dietitian. A product with several unclear ingredients is not a controlled single-group reintroduction challenge. Our guide to the three steps of the low FODMAP diet explains how that works.

When to talk to a doctor

Label reading is for people managing symptoms that have already been assessed. See a doctor (GP) first if you haven't been diagnosed, and promptly if you notice blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, a persistent change in bowel habit, or symptoms that wake you at night [1][2]. And if you find yourself avoiding more and more foods, that's a good moment to ask for a dietitian referral [12].

Frequently asked questions

Is garlic-infused oil the same as garlic on a label?

No. Monash explains that fructans dissolve in water but not oil. An infused oil with the garlic pieces removed differs from a product containing garlic solids. Check the actual ingredients and any tested serving guidance [13].

Does "gluten-free" mean low FODMAP?

Not necessarily. Gluten-free products can still contain inulin, honey, polyols or other FODMAP sources [3]. Gluten is a protein; FODMAPs are carbohydrates.

Are all sweeteners polyols?

No. Polyols are recognisable by names ending in "-itol" (plus isomalt) and by numbers such as 420, 421, 953, 965, 966 and 967 [3].
