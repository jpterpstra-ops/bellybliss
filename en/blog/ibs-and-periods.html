IBS and your period: why gut symptoms can change across your cycle

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

If your IBS seems to get louder in the days before or during your period, you're not imagining it. Research has found that some people with IBS experience changes in symptoms around menstruation; hormone changes may play a part [2][3]. For many people, knowing this is the first step to planning around it rather than being caught off guard every month.

This article explains what research has found, which factors may be involved, practical things you can try, and the symptoms that deserve a doctor's attention.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

What the research shows

Studies following women with IBS across the cycle provide useful observations, but do not establish an individual cause.

  • In a small study of women with IBS published in Gut, abdominal pain and bloating were worse at menstruation than in most other phases of the cycle, bowel movements were more frequent, and stools were harder in the luteal phase (the second half of the cycle, after ovulation) [2]. The researchers also found that the gut was more sensitive during menstruation in women with IBS, something they did not see in healthy women [2].
  • A review of the literature concluded that women with IBS often report more gastrointestinal symptoms around menses, although patterns vary between individuals [3].

The research is still limited. Studies are often small, and no one can predict from a calendar exactly how you will feel. But the general finding (more symptoms around your period for many people with IBS) is consistent.

Why it might happen

Hormones

Oestrogen (estrogen) and progesterone rise and fall across the cycle, and may influence gut function [3]. Hormonal effects on gut movement and sensitivity are being studied; they do not explain every person’s symptoms in the same way [2][3].

Prostaglandins

Around menstruation the body releases prostaglandins, which help the womb contract. They may also stimulate the bowel, which could contribute to looser, more frequent stools and cramping in the first days of a period [2].

A more sensitive gut

In IBS, the gut can be more sensitive to normal sensations. The Gut study suggests this sensitivity may increase during menstruation [2], which could make the same amount of gas or movement feel more uncomfortable.

Everything else that changes

Sleep, appetite, cravings, stress, and pain medication often shift around the same time. If period pain means more coffee, skipped meals or a different diet for a few days, those changes can affect your gut too. This is why it helps to record them.

Bloated or distended?

Monash University distinguishes between bloating, the feeling of pressure or swelling, and distension, a measurable increase in the size of your tummy [7]. Both are common in IBS, and both can change across the cycle. Noting which one you have (does it feel tight, or do your clothes actually fit differently?) makes your diary more precise and helps your doctor understand what you mean.

Track it for two or three cycles

Patterns are personal, so the most useful evidence is your own. For two or three cycles, note:

  • the day of your cycle (day 1 = first day of bleeding),
  • your main gut symptoms and how severe they are,
  • what you ate, how you slept and how stressed you felt.

Our IBS food and symptom diary guide includes a template with a column for cycle day. After a few months you may see that, for example, bloating builds in the week before your period, or that looser stools cluster around days 1–2. That's useful to know, and useful to show your doctor.

Things you can try

There's no special "period diet" for IBS with strong evidence behind it, but general IBS advice can be timed around the days you know are harder:

  • Keep meals regular and avoid long gaps, as NICE recommends for IBS in general [4].
  • Be gentle with known personal triggers in the days when your gut is most sensitive. If you're working with a dietitian on the low FODMAP diet, your reintroduction results can tell you which foods matter most for you.
  • Mind the extras: coffee, alcohol and fizzy drinks are on NICE's list of things to limit for IBS [4].
  • Make space for rest and stress relief. Stress can affect IBS symptoms [1]. Gentle movement or a breathing break may be ways to pause if they feel comfortable; they are not a treatment for the underlying cause of pain. See IBS and stress.
  • Ask about pain relief. Some pain medicines can trigger gut symptoms [1]. A pharmacist or doctor can help you choose.

When it might not be IBS

Gut symptoms around your period are common, but some conditions can look similar. Talk to a doctor (GP) if:

  • your period pain is severe, stops you from doing normal things, or painkillers don't help;
  • you have pain during or after sex, or pain when you poo or pee during your period;
  • you feel bloated or have a swollen tummy most days (roughly 12 or more times a month), or you feel full quickly — the NHS lists this among symptoms that should be checked, even though they're usually caused by something less serious [5];
  • you notice blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, or bleeding between periods or after sex [4][5].

Endometriosis, for example, can cause painful periods and bowel symptoms, and is sometimes mistaken for IBS [6]. A doctor can help work out what's going on.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my IBS worse before my period?

Hormonal changes may influence gut movement and sensitivity, but the research does not explain every person’s pattern. Some people report more constipation or bloating before a period [2][3]. Record your experience and discuss persistent changes with your doctor.

Can your period cause IBS-like diarrhoea?

Looser, more frequent stools around menstruation are common, also in people without IBS. Prostaglandins are a likely contributor [2].

Does hormonal contraception change IBS symptoms?

Research is limited and results are mixed. If you notice changes after starting or stopping contraception, discuss it with your doctor.
