IBS food and symptom diary: what to record (with a free template)

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 6 min read

"Keep a food diary" is one of the first things you hear after an IBS diagnosis. The NHS suggests it, and so does the British Dietetic Association [1][2]. What people are rarely told is how to keep one so that it's useful a few weeks later, for you and for whoever you show it to.

This guide covers what to record, how long to keep going, how to read it without jumping to conclusions, and a simple template you can print or copy.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

Why a diary helps

IBS symptoms tend to come and go, and food is only one of several things that can play a part. Stress, sleep, your menstrual cycle, medicines and infections can all matter [3][4]. Memory is unreliable for this: after a bad day, the most recent meal tends to get the blame.

A diary replaces "I think it was the pasta" with a record. An NHS hospital leaflet makes two useful points: trigger foods can cause symptoms straight away or up to 48 hours later, and you shouldn't cut out food groups without speaking to a healthcare professional or dietitian [5]. Both are reasons to write things down rather than guess.

What to record

Keep it short enough that you'll actually do it. A good entry covers:

Food and drink

  • What you ate and drank, including snacks, sauces, gum, coffee and alcohol.
  • Roughly how much. "Small bowl" or "two slices" is enough; portion size changes how many FODMAPs you get [6].
  • Packaged products by name, so you can check the ingredient list later. Our guide to hidden FODMAPs on labels explains what to look for.

Symptoms

  • Type: pain, bloating, wind, diarrhoea (diarrhea), constipation, urgency, nausea.
  • Severity: a simple 0–10 scale works well.
  • Timing: when it started and how long it lasted.

Bowel movements

Many clinicians use the Bristol Stool Form Scale (types 1 to 7) because it gives a shared vocabulary for stool consistency [7]. Noting the type is quicker and clearer than describing it.

Context

  • Stress and sleep: a one-word note ("rough night", "deadline") is plenty. See our piece on IBS and stress.
  • Menstrual cycle: if you have periods, note the day of your cycle. Symptoms can shift around menstruation [3][8]. More in IBS and your period.
  • Medicines and supplements, including probiotics and fibre (fiber) supplements.
  • Exercise and meals skipped or eaten late.

How long to keep it

Agree a recording period with your clinician. There is no universal minimum that establishes a trigger. The NHS leaflet mentioned above uses a four-week format [5]. If you're doing a structured low FODMAP trial, your dietitian will usually ask you to keep recording throughout, especially during reintroduction, where you test one FODMAP group at a time [6].

Reading your diary without over-reading it

A diary shows associations, not proof. A food that appears on bad days might be a trigger, or it might just be something you eat often. A few habits keep you honest:

  • Look for repeats. One bad afternoon after a food is a note; a repeated association is worth discussing with your clinician, without assuming a cause.
  • Timing varies. The Leicester leaflet notes that symptoms can occur immediately or up to 48 hours later; this is not a precise causal window for every person [5].
  • Change one thing at a time, as the BDA recommends, so you can see what helped [2].
  • Don't keep shrinking your diet. If your list of "avoid" foods keeps growing, talk to your GP or a dietitian. Unnecessary restriction can affect nutrition and your relationship with food [9].

Common diary mistakes

  • Writing it up at the end of the day. Details fade quickly. A quick note right after eating is more reliable than a full reconstruction at night.
  • Only recording bad days. Good days are just as informative: they show what you ate when things went well.
  • Leaving out drinks and "little things". Coffee, a sugar-free mint or a splash of sauce can matter.
  • Changing several things at once. If you start a new supplement, change your breakfast and sleep more in the same week, you won't know what made the difference [2].
  • Drawing conclusions after a few days. Review patterns with your clinician rather than using a fixed number of entries as proof. Do not delay medical advice for concerning symptoms.

Bring it to your appointment

A diary is most useful when someone with expertise looks at it with you. Before seeing your GP or dietitian, highlight your main symptoms, the worst days, and any questions. Mention red-flag symptoms straight away rather than waiting for the diary to show them: blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, a persistent change in bowel habit (especially over 60), or a family history of bowel or ovarian cancer [2][10].

Frequently asked questions

Is there an NHS IBS food diary template?

Several NHS trusts publish printable food and symptom diaries, such as the one from University Hospitals of Leicester [5]. The independent BellyBliss template above is not an NHS form or an NHS-endorsed tool.

Should I record everything I eat?

Ideally, yes, including drinks, snacks and sauces. If that feels like too much, record main meals and anything new or unusual.

Can a food diary diagnose IBS?

No. IBS is diagnosed by a doctor after other conditions have been considered [10]. A diary supports that conversation.
