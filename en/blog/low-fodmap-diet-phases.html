IBS & low FODMAP

The low FODMAP diet in three steps: restriction, reintroduction, personalisation

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

The low FODMAP diet is often described as a list of foods to avoid. That's only the first few weeks of it. As developed by Monash University, it's a three-step process, and the goal is to end up eating as varied a diet as your gut allows, not to cut foods out for good [1].

This guide explains each step, how long it usually takes, where a diary fits in, and why it's worth doing with a dietitian.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

Before you start

  • Get a diagnosis. The diet is designed to help manage the symptoms of medically diagnosed IBS [1]. Symptoms like bloating and changes in bowel habit can have other causes, and NICE recommends basic tests (including a blood test for coeliac disease) when IBS is suspected [2].
  • Try first-line advice. In the UK, NICE and the BDA start with general diet and lifestyle advice, such as regular meals and adjusting fibre, caffeine, alcohol and fizzy drinks. If symptoms persist, NICE says that further dietary advice, including the low FODMAP diet, should only be given by a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [2][3]. The American College of Gastroenterology also recommends a limited trial of a low FODMAP diet for IBS symptoms [4].
  • Find a dietitian. In the UK you can ask your GP for an NHS referral or search the BDA's directory; in Australia and the US, look for a dietitian with FODMAP training [3].

The diet isn't suitable for everyone. If you have a history of disordered eating, or you notice that food is becoming a source of anxiety, tell your doctor or dietitian before you begin [5].

Step 1: restriction (2–6 weeks)

For a short period, you swap high FODMAP foods for lower FODMAP alternatives. Monash advises following this step for 2 to 6 weeks [1]. This means suitable substitutions, not skipping meals. Your dietitian can help choose alternatives and portions; gluten-free bread is not automatically low FODMAP.

Packaged food is where FODMAPs most often slip through, usually as garlic, onion, inulin or polyols in the ingredient list. Our guide to hidden FODMAPs on food labels covers the names to watch.

If your symptoms improve, move on to step 2. If they don't, your symptoms may not be FODMAP-sensitive, and there's no point staying restricted. Monash suggests considering other approaches with your health professional, such as stress reduction, gut-directed hypnotherapy, fibre supplements or medicines [1].

Step 2: reintroduction (several weeks)

Reintroduction helps avoid keeping unnecessary restrictions. You keep eating low FODMAP as a baseline, while testing one FODMAP group at a time.

How a challenge works

Monash describes a challenge as eating a food rich in only one FODMAP group daily for three days while monitoring symptoms [1]. For example, milk is used to test lactose because it doesn't contain other FODMAPs. Between challenges you return to your baseline until symptoms have settled.

Which groups get tested

Dietitians typically work through the subgroups one by one: fructans (from grains, and from vegetables such as onion and garlic), GOS, lactose, excess fructose, sorbitol and mannitol [6]. The order and portion sizes vary, so follow your dietitian's plan. Monash also publishes a step-2 guide [6].

Why a diary matters here

Recording each test helps you and your dietitian interpret it: what you ate, how much, and how you felt over the next day or two. Monash suggests a paper diary or an app diary for this [1]. Our food and symptom diary guide has a template.

Step 3: personalisation (long term)

In step 3 you bring back as many foods as possible, keeping restrictions only for the FODMAPs and amounts that clearly trigger your symptoms [1]. Many people find they tolerate some groups well, others only in small portions, and only one or two poorly. The aim is a diet that's as varied and nutritionally balanced as possible, which is also better for your gut bacteria, because many high FODMAP foods are good sources of prebiotic fibre [1].

Your tolerance can change over time, so it's reasonable to re-test foods later with your dietitian.

Questions to ask your dietitian

  • Which foods should I swap first, and which can I keep?
  • How long should I stay in step 1 in my situation?
  • In which order will we test the FODMAP groups, and with what portions?
  • What should I do if a challenge gives me symptoms halfway through?
  • How do I keep enough fibre (fiber), calcium and variety while restricting?
  • When should we re-test foods I didn't tolerate?

Making it work day to day

  • Plan the first week. Pick a handful of easy meals before you start.
  • Check labels, not just foods. Sauces, stock, seasonings and "high fibre" products are the usual suspects.
  • Watch portion sizes. Some foods are fine in a small serving and not in a larger one [7].
  • Don't stay in step 1. Long-term restriction isn't the goal, and it can make eating unnecessarily stressful [5].

Tools can take some of the effort out of it. A lab-based database such as the Monash FODMAP app gives tested values and serving sizes [1]. BellyBliss focuses on packaged products and your own records: you can scan a barcode or photograph an ingredient list to see possible FODMAP concerns, record food and symptoms in the diary during reintroduction, and afterwards set your personal tolerances per FODMAP group, including "Small portion". The app doesn't set a safe amount for you; your results and your dietitian's advice do.

Frequently asked questions

How long does the whole low FODMAP diet take?

Monash describes 2–6 weeks for the first step [1]. Reintroduction takes several weeks and is individual; personalisation continues long term. Agree the timing with your dietitian.

Can I do the low FODMAP diet without a dietitian?

NICE advises that it be given by a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [2]. A dietitian helps you avoid unnecessary restriction and keep your diet balanced.

Is low FODMAP the same as gluten-free?

No. Wheat is limited in step 1 because of its fructans, not its gluten. Many people reintroduce wheat successfully.
