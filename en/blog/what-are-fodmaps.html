IBS & low FODMAP

What are FODMAPs, and how do they relate to IBS?

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

FODMAP is one of those terms that suddenly seems to be everywhere: on food blogs, in apps and in conversations about a sensitive gut. It sounds technical, but the basic idea does not require a chemistry lesson.

This guide is for understanding the term, not for starting a restrictive diet tomorrow or deciding that you have irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). IBS is diagnosed by a doctor. The Dutch digestive health organisation MDL Fonds describes abdominal pain and changes in bowel habits without a visible abnormality explaining them. Symptoms vary between people and from day to day.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

What the letters stand for

Monash University describes FODMAPs as a group of short-chain carbohydrates that are not fully digested or absorbed in the small intestine.

  • Fermentable: gut bacteria can ferment these carbohydrates, producing gas.
  • Oligosaccharides: fructans and galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS). Examples include wheat, rye, onion, garlic and pulses.
  • Disaccharides: lactose, the sugar in milk and some dairy products.
  • Monosaccharides: fructose, found in foods such as honey, apples and some syrups.
  • And polyols: sugar alcohols such as sorbitol and mannitol, found in some fruit and used as sweeteners.

You do not need to memorise every term. The acronym groups together carbohydrates found in quite different foods: an apple, a bulb of garlic, a glass of milk or sugar-free chewing gum.

What happens in the gut

Monash explains two processes. FODMAPs draw water into the small intestine. Further along, bacteria in the large intestine use them as fuel and produce gas. The extra water and gas can stretch the bowel.

This is not unique to people with IBS. A more sensitive gut may respond to that stretching with pain, bloating, wind or changes in bowel habits. Some people experience looser stools, some constipation, and others alternate between them.

That gives bloating after eating some context, but food is only part of the picture. Stress can also make symptoms worse. That does not mean IBS is imaginary or that you are causing your own symptoms. Read more about IBS and stress.

You cannot tell a food’s FODMAP content by looking at it

FODMAPs occur in vegetables, fruit, bread, dairy, nuts, pulses and sweets. Monash makes an important distinction: a food’s FODMAP content cannot reliably be guessed. Laboratory testing is needed. Ripeness, variety and processing can all affect the amount.

Portions matter too. A small amount of the same food may be different from a larger serving. This is not a reason to calculate everything to the last decimal place. It is a reason not to treat an ingredient name as a permanent red stamp on an entire food group.

Examples towards the higher end of Monash’s overview include onion, garlic, many pulses, honey, apples, pears and ordinary cow’s milk. Examples towards the lower end include carrot, potato, kiwi, hard cheese and eggs. These examples are not a personal shopping list or permission to avoid everything else. Your own tolerance matters. How FODMAPs relate to IBS

FODMAP is not another name for IBS. IBS is a diagnosis involving recurring abdominal pain and altered bowel habits. FODMAPs are carbohydrates. For some people with IBS, these carbohydrates contribute to bloating, pain or changes in stools; for others, they may not be the main factor.

A high- and low-FODMAP food chart cannot diagnose IBS. If you are unsure what your symptoms mean, start with a doctor. Our guide to IBS symptoms explains common features and reasons to seek medical advice. A dietitian can then help decide whether a low FODMAP approach is appropriate.

Low FODMAP is not the same as gluten-free

Wheat bread often comes up in conversations about IBS, followed quickly by the word gluten. Gluten is a protein. The FODMAPs in wheat discussed here are fructans, which are carbohydrates. Coeliac disease is a separate condition involving gluten and needs medical assessment.

Thuisarts, the Dutch patient information service from general practitioners, says that a gluten-free diet is not routinely needed for IBS. A gluten-free biscuit may still contain honey, inulin or a polyol sweetener. Equally, a product is not automatically unsuitable because it contains wheat. Portion size and the rest of the ingredients matter. See hidden FODMAPs on food labels.

A way to investigate, not a lifelong exclusion list

Monash describes the low FODMAP approach for people whose IBS has been diagnosed by a doctor. Similar symptoms can occur with other conditions, including coeliac disease or bowel inflammation. Giving yourself a label and cutting out several foods can mean missing something that needs investigation.

Thuisarts warns that excluding several foods without a dietitian can leave you short of nutrients or energy, or anxious about trying them again. A list of higher-FODMAP foods is a research tool, not a definition of what you can eat for life. A chart alone cannot tell you whether an apple triggers your symptoms.

A dietitian helps you investigate while keeping your diet adequate and varied. The process includes three phases, including bringing foods back.

One thing you can do without starting a diet is record what you ate and how your gut felt later. A FODMAP scanner can help identify names on the label alongside those notes. Patterns can make a conversation more useful; they do not prove that a food caused your symptoms.

Frequently asked questions

What are FODMAPs?

FODMAPs are short-chain carbohydrates that are not fully absorbed in the small intestine. Bacteria in the large intestine can ferment them and produce gas. People differ in how sensitive they are to these effects.

What is the low FODMAP diet?

It is a temporary, dietitian-supported approach to exploring which FODMAP groups may contribute to IBS symptoms. The process often takes a few months, is not a weight-loss plan and does not work for everyone.

Is low FODMAP the same as gluten-free?

No. Gluten is a protein; fructans, the FODMAPs discussed in wheat, are carbohydrates. Gluten-free foods can still contain FODMAPs. IBS alone does not mean you need a gluten-free diet.
