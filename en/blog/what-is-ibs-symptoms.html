What is IBS? Symptoms and when to speak to a doctor

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

You may first hear about IBS from a doctor, a friend or a conversation about whether your gut symptoms are normal. Having a name can be reassuring. It can also leave you wondering what to do next.

This guide explains what irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is, symptoms often associated with it and reasons to speak to a doctor. A familiar story or an online list does not establish a diagnosis. If food choices are becoming increasingly restricted, involve a dietitian as well.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

What IBS is, and what it is not

Thuisarts, the Dutch GP patient information service, describes IBS as frequent abdominal pain with altered bowel habits, sometimes accompanied by wind or bloating. The bowel can work or respond differently even without visible inflammation. Older descriptions sometimes used the term “spastic colon”. Thuisarts estimates that one in 25 people in the Netherlands has IBS and notes that it is more common in women. This is a Dutch estimate, not a worldwide prevalence figure.

The exact causes are not fully understood. Increased gut sensitivity, changes in bowel movement and changes in gut bacteria, for example after a stomach infection, may be involved. Passing gas or stool may be uncomfortable in a sensitive bowel even when the same process would not hurt someone else.

MDL Fonds also describes the ongoing communication between the gut and brain. The older idea that IBS is simply imagined or a personal psychological failing is misleading. Stress and anxiety can nevertheless affect physical symptoms. Our article on IBS and stress explores that connection without blame.

IBS is not another name for FODMAP. FODMAPs are carbohydrates that may contribute to symptoms for some people. Read what FODMAPs are; a food chart cannot diagnose IBS.

Thuisarts explains that IBS itself is not dangerous and does not increase the risk of bowel inflammation or bowel cancer. That reassurance applies to the diagnosed condition, not to every new symptom. Symptoms often persist, although advice and care may help make them more manageable.

A doctor usually starts with questions and an abdominal examination. A colonoscopy does not confirm IBS merely by how the bowel looks and is not automatically required. If another condition is suspected, the doctor decides what investigations are appropriate. Do not use this description to rule out tests that have been recommended for you.

Common IBS symptoms

Symptoms can vary between people and between days. Abdominal pain or cramps may move around. The abdomen may feel uncomfortable, bloated or visibly larger. Symptoms can change after a bowel movement or after eating.

Stools may be hard and dry, or loose and frequent. Constipation and diarrhoea can alternate. Mucus and wind can also occur.

MDL Fonds describes bloating as often worse later in the day and after meals, sometimes without the bowel being particularly full. Burping, wind and rumbling may accompany it. Pain may ease after passing stool or wind. None of these patterns identifies one food as the cause.

There is no single treatment that works for everyone. Complete absence of symptoms is not always achievable, but a doctor or dietitian can help explore ways to reduce their impact. See bloating after eating and the wider guide to possible causes of bloating.

IBS and women’s health

IBS being more common in women does not make the symptoms less important. Menstrual changes can overlap with gut symptoms. MDL Fonds describes a tendency towards constipation before a period and looser stools during it, with some people with IBS noticing a stronger change. Read IBS and periods for what to record.

Endometriosis is a separate condition involving tissue resembling the lining of the womb outside the womb. Its symptoms can resemble IBS, particularly around menstruation. A doctor needs to assess that possibility. Severe pain or bleeding that stops you getting through the day warrants medical advice.

Thuisarts encourages staying involved in work and social activities where possible. If pain or fear of being far from a toilet is limiting what you can do, tell your doctor rather than treating it as something you must manage alone.

When to speak to a doctor

If you have not been diagnosed, start with a medical assessment. Thuisarts lists changes that should not simply be attributed to IBS:

  • Blood in or around your stools.
  • A sudden major change in how often you pass stool.
  • A sudden marked change in stool consistency.
  • Frequent urges to go without passing anything.
  • Unexplained weight loss.
  • Bowel symptoms first appearing at age 50 or older.

MDL Fonds also highlights fever. The Dutch general practice guideline discusses night-time diarrhoea, persistent changes towards looser or more frequent stools, and a parent or sibling with inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease or certain cancers at a younger age.

These are reasons for a doctor to assess the situation, not a checklist for diagnosing yourself. Do not dismiss blood, fever or night-time symptoms as a busy week or something you ate. Severe abdominal pain with fever needs urgent medical advice; contact the appropriate urgent-care service where you live.

What you can record yourself

A short diary can make a consultation more concrete, whether you have a diagnosis or are awaiting one. You might note food, gut symptoms, bowel movements, sleep, stress and your cycle where relevant. One entry does not prove a cause.

You can use paper. BellyBliss can also hold food and gut-feeling entries alongside ingredient scans. Read how the scanner works. It does not diagnose IBS.

Thuisarts suggests that one or two repeatedly troublesome foods can be reduced for a few weeks to see what happens. If many foods seem involved, do not remove them all at once. A dietitian helps maintain adequate nutrition; too little food or fibre can make matters worse. See what to eat with IBS for the first steps.

Frequently asked questions

What is IBS?

Irritable bowel syndrome involves recurring abdominal pain and altered bowel habits, sometimes with wind or bloating. The bowel may function or respond differently without visible inflammation. A doctor makes the diagnosis.

Is IBS dangerous?

Thuisarts explains that diagnosed IBS itself does not increase the risk of bowel inflammation or bowel cancer. Blood in stools, unexplained weight loss, fever, night-time symptoms or symptoms starting later in life still need medical assessment.

Can I diagnose IBS myself?

No. A familiar symptom pattern, a food list or an app cannot diagnose IBS. Make an appointment with a doctor if you are unsure what your symptoms mean.
