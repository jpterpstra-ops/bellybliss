IBS & low FODMAP

What to eat with IBS: start with the basics

Updated on 19 November 2026 · About 5 min read

Search for “what to eat with IBS” and you will quickly find a list: green foods, red foods and a reassuring picture of a plate. Then another list contradicts it, and your gut is uncomfortable again the next day. Doctors and dietitians tend to start somewhere less dramatic: regular, adequate meals before more targeted changes.

A doctor diagnoses IBS. This article is not a menu or a list of forbidden foods. It follows the first steps described by Thuisarts, the Netherlands Nutrition Centre and the Dutch Association of Dietitians.

BellyBliss is a support tool, not medical advice. Discuss symptoms and dietary changes with a doctor or dietitian.

There is no IBS food list that works for everyone

Thuisarts explains that no single food or drink causes symptoms in everyone with IBS. Symptoms after eating something do not automatically mean an allergy. You may tolerate a small amount differently from a large portion.

Some people notice symptoms with cabbage, onion, garlic, hot spices, fatty foods, alcohol, coffee, fizzy drinks, sweeteners, milk or wheat bran. These are examples of individual experiences, not a list to avoid starting tomorrow.

The Dutch Association of Dietitians makes the same point: a food that troubles one person may be fine for someone else. Unnecessary restrictions can eventually lead to nutritional shortfalls.

FODMAP is neither another name for IBS nor a universal shopping list. Read what FODMAPs are and our guide to food lists, labels and portions in Dutch supermarkets. A tick on a chart cannot establish your personal threshold.

Practical first steps with food

Thuisarts starts with regular meals, rather than a special diet. Its guidance describes three balanced meals at regular times, including vegetables and fruit, rather than grazing all day or eating nothing until a heavy evening meal. The Dutch general practice guideline also puts a more regular eating pattern first. Eating pace and chewing are further points to discuss.

Fibre helps the bowel manage stool consistency. It holds water, which can help soften hard stools, and can also bind water in loose stools. Sources include vegetables, fruit, potatoes, wholegrain foods, pulses, nuts and seeds. Meat, cheese and eggs do not provide fibre.

MDL Fonds notes that increasing fibre may initially bring more wind or a fuller feeling. Increase it gradually, and consider fluid intake as well. Not drinking enough alongside extra fibre can make constipation worse.

The Dutch guidance recommends 1.5 to 2 litres of fluid a day, favouring water or tea without sugar or sweeteners and limiting sugary drinks such as juice and soft drinks. These are general recommendations to discuss in the context of your own care.

If bowel problems continue despite enough dietary fibre, Thuisarts discusses plain psyllium fibre as an option. How it is used depends on the problem being addressed. Discuss this with a doctor or dietitian rather than buying a supplement and guessing.

Movement is another part of the guidance, such as a daily half-hour walk or cycle. It is a lifestyle measure, not a promise that symptoms will disappear. Discuss activity that is difficult because of symptoms with your healthcare professional.

When one food keeps appearing in your notes

If one or two foods repeatedly seem linked to symptoms, Thuisarts suggests seeing what happens when you have less of them, or leave them out, for a few weeks. Record what changed and what happened to your symptoms so you have something more useful than a general impression.

If many different foods seem involved, do not stop eating them all without support. Thuisarts describes the risks: too little energy, vitamins or minerals, and anxiety about trying foods again. Some people develop disordered eating. This is a point to involve a dietitian, not make a stricter list. Your doctor can help arrange support.

A scanner can help read ingredient names such as onion, garlic or a sweetener. See how BellyBliss works. An ingredient flag is a consideration, not proof that it will cause symptoms for you.

When to consider a dietitian and a low FODMAP approach

If the usual advice does not help enough and symptoms remain troublesome, Thuisarts describes a low FODMAP diet as a possible next step. It is a temporary way to investigate which carbohydrate groups may contribute to symptoms, usually over a few months, with a dietitian.

It is not a weight-loss plan and does not guarantee improvement. The Netherlands Nutrition Centre describes evidence of benefit for some people with IBS, while emphasising that FODMAPs occur in many ordinary staple foods. The complexity is a reason to involve a dietitian experienced in digestive conditions.

The Dutch Association of Dietitians also makes clear that a diet does not cure IBS. Symptoms may become more manageable for some people, but responses differ.

The three-phase approach includes expanding your diet again. Do not remain in the strictest weeks because reintroduction feels worrying. Helping you regain variety is part of the dietitian’s role.

A food diary gives you something concrete to discuss

Notes show what happened in your own days. You might record the time, food, gut symptoms, bowel movements, sleep, stress and your cycle where relevant. A busy day can look like a reaction to lunch if the rest of the picture is missing. Read about IBS and stress and IBS and periods.

BellyBliss can place food and gut-feeling entries alongside each other. An association in those records does not prove that the food caused the symptom. It can start a more useful conversation with a doctor or dietitian. The broader picture is explained in what IBS is.

Do not wait for a complete diary before contacting a doctor about blood in stools, unintended weight loss, fever, night-time diarrhoea, symptoms beginning after age 50 or a close family history of relevant bowel disease or bowel cancer.

Frequently asked questions

Is there one food list for everyone with IBS?

No. People differ in which foods affect their symptoms. Symptoms after a food do not automatically mean an allergy, and a small serving may be tolerated differently from a large one.

What can I try first?

The Dutch guidance starts with regular meals, fibre and 1.5 to 2 litres of fluid a day. Increase fibre gradually. If only one or two foods repeatedly seem involved, a short period of reducing them may help you investigate. Improvement is not guaranteed.

When should I involve a dietitian?

Seek support when many foods seem involved or eating a balanced diet is difficult. A restrictive low FODMAP diet should be undertaken with a dietitian. It is temporary and does not cure IBS.
